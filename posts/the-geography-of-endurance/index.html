The Geography of Endurance: Why Maritime Coalitions Outlast Land Empires

2026-08-11

In the study of grand strategy, scholars often fall into the trap of measuring national power by square miles and standing armies. As Sarah Paine and the classic strategists like Mahan, Mackinder, and Spykman observed, geography dictates the terms of endurance long before the first shot is fired.

The core divide in geopolitics is not merely East versus West, but maritime versus continental. Continental empires possess terrifying short-term mobilization power and interior lines of communication. Yet over the long economic and military horizon, maritime coalitions systematically outlast them.

The Economics of the Global Commons

Bulk maritime transport operates at a fraction of the energy and infrastructure cost of overland transit. More importantly, open trade networks aggregate sovereign industrial outputs across disparate allies. When nations pool resources via ocean-going commerce, they build an economic depth that no single continental hegemon—relying on coercive extraction from contiguous peripheries—can replicate.

A continental empire draws from its immediate borders. A maritime coalition draws from the world.

Strategic Depth vs. Interior Lines

Consider the historical pattern: Athens and the Delian League versus Sparta; Great Britain and its allies versus Napoleonic France, Wilhelmine Germany, and the Soviet Union. In each case, the continental power enjoyed interior lines of communication, allowing rapid troop movements across land borders.

Yet, maritime coalitions consistently survived the initial shock through financial depth, sovereign credit generation, and external resource access. By maintaining command of the sea, they converted global commerce into war-fighting capital. Endurance in protracted attrition warfare is fundamentally a function of economic elasticity, which open oceanic trade uniquely provides.

The Continental Counter-Case: Interior Defense and A2/AD Realities

A rigorous examination must confront the strongest opposing case. Land powers enjoy undeniable advantages: interior lines of communication are inherently more defensible against blockade, and autarkic resource access renders continental states immune to interdiction in narrow sea lanes like the English Channel or the Malacca Strait.

Furthermore, modern anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) weapon systems, hypersonic missiles, and cyber threats targeting undersea cables compress traditional oceanic sanctuaries. Skeptics argue that these technologies erode the historical insulation of sea powers, neutralizing the economic advantage of maritime trade.

Yet, maritime coalitions adapt. By decentralizing supply chains, hardening digital infrastructure, and securing littoral choke points, sea-power networks maintain their structural elasticity.

Conclusion: The Future of Maritime Grand Strategy

Great power longevity is determined not by raw landmass, but by access to the global commons and the trust underpinning coalition structures. As contemporary competition unfolds across contested logistics corridors, the lesson from maritime history remains sharp: land power conquers territory, but sea power sustains endurance.
