The Geography of Endurance: Why Maritime Coalitions Outlast Land Empires

2026-08-11

  • geopolitics
  • grand-strategy
  • maritime-power

Athens had the best navy of the ancient world, and it still lost the Peloponnesian War. The sea is not a shield; it is a running account, and a coalition that stops paying into it is one bad battle from the end. As Sarah Paine and the classic strategists Mahan, Mackinder, and Spykman observed, geography dictates the terms of endurance long before the first shot is fired.

The core divide in geopolitics is maritime versus continental, not East versus West. Continental empires possess enormous short-term mobilization power and interior lines of communication. Yet over the long economic and military horizon, maritime coalitions systematically outlast them.

The asymmetry keeps repeating because logistics does not care who holds more territory. An army that moves fast inside its own borders runs out of steam at the border; a navy that holds the sea lanes keeps feeding a coalition for decades. The long wars of the modern era keep turning on that distinction, and it decides who still has cash and food when the other side is exhausted.

The Economics of the Global Commons

Bulk maritime transport operates at a fraction of the energy and infrastructure cost of overland transit. A ton of freight moves across the ocean for one to three cents a mile, against two to five by rail and eight or more by truck, and the ocean needs no paving, no rail bed, no maintenance crews. Open trade networks aggregate sovereign industrial outputs across disparate allies. When nations pool resources via ocean-going commerce, they build an economic depth that no single continental hegemon can replicate, because it pays for every mile of road and every depot out of its own coerced surplus.

The record backs the geography. Britain built its 19th-century power importing wheat and cotton it could not grow, financed on credit raised in London against future trade; the United States fed Europe in two world wars by convoy across the Atlantic. A land power must build the road to reach its next province; a maritime coalition simply points the next ship. The deeper advantage is the credit itself. An open economy can borrow against commerce it has not yet earned, which is why maritime coalitions fight on money they do not yet have while land empires spend what they have already taken.

A continental empire draws from its immediate borders. A maritime coalition draws from the world.

Strategic Depth vs. Interior Lines

Consider the historical pattern: Athens and the Delian League versus Sparta; Great Britain and its allies versus Napoleonic France, Wilhelmine Germany, and the Soviet Union. In each case, the continental power enjoyed interior lines of communication, allowing rapid troop movements across land borders.

Yet maritime coalitions consistently survived the initial shock through financial depth and external resource access. By maintaining command of the sea, they converted global commerce into war-fighting capital. Endurance in attrition warfare comes down to economic elasticity, the capacity to keep paying and feeding a war after the initial push stalls, and open oceanic trade uniquely provides it.

The mechanism repeats. Against Napoleon, Britain kept its fleet at sea and outspent the French Empire on credit raised in London, and at Trafalgar in 1805 it ended the Franco-Spanish fleet as a rival. Napoleon answered with the Continental System, closing Europe to British goods; the scheme failed on its own arithmetic, because the Royal Navy was taxing French trade while France could only starve British trade on paper.

In both world wars, Britain fought on the same terms. Against Wilhelmine Germany, the Royal Navy settled into the blockade that cut Germany’s imports to a fifth of their pre-war volume by 1918. Germany’s answer in the second war was to send submarines to sever Britain’s lifeline, and again the convoy system kept the lanes open through the turning point of May 1943. Athens is the same story in miniature: Delian League tribute and the grain route through the Hellespont funded the walls and triremes that outlasted a generation of Spartan invasions.

The Cold War ran the same play on a larger board. The Soviet Union was the purest land power of all, the world’s largest country, able to mass armies inside its own territory and shift them along interior rail lines at will. Yet it spent 40 years in a command economy that could not match Western productive capacity. NATO never had to win on the ground; it kept trading and kept the Atlantic lanes open, and in 1991 the Soviet system collapsed under that economic weight.

The Continental Counter-Case: Interior Defense and A2/AD Realities

The strongest case for land power deserves a hearing. Land powers enjoy undeniable advantages: interior lines of communication are inherently more defensible against blockade, and autarkic resource access renders continental states immune to interdiction in narrow sea lanes like the English Channel or the Malacca Strait.

Furthermore, modern anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) weapon systems, hypersonic missiles, and cyber threats targeting undersea cables compress traditional oceanic sanctuaries. Skeptics argue that these technologies erode the historical insulation of maritime coalitions, neutralizing the economic advantage of maritime trade. The sea lanes a coalition depends on are fixed, while the weapons pointed at them keep getting cheaper.

The record offers one stubborn counterexample: Athens, the archetype of the maritime coalition, eventually lost. When Persia financed a Spartan fleet that caught the Athenian navy beached at Aegospotami in 405 BC, a coalition that had survived a generation of land invasions collapsed within months. Command of the sea must be re-earned every day; lose it once, and economic depth stops mattering. The Delian League’s accumulated wealth did not save the city once the grain route through the Hellespont was cut.

Yet maritime coalitions adapt. By decentralizing supply chains and hardening the digital and littoral infrastructure those chains depend on, maritime coalitions keep their structural elasticity. The adaptation is already visible. Supply chains that once ran on single just-in-time routes are diversifying across ports; undersea cables are getting redundant paths and satellite backup; and navies are pre-positioning fuel, munitions, and repair capacity closer to the contested lanes. None of this restores the old monopoly on safety. It restores the open sea, which is what the coalition actually needs.

Conclusion: The Future of Maritime Grand Strategy

Great power longevity comes down to access to the global commons and the trust underpinning coalition structures, not raw landmass. Trust is what lets an alliance of sovereign states act like one economy, sharing ports and pipelines in a crisis. As the current rivalry tests the same corridors, the margin is unchanged: maritime coalitions can keep paying for the war after land empires run out of money to pay for theirs. Sea power doesn’t conquer. It endures.
