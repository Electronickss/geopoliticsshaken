The Gated Seas: When Open Water Stops Being Open

2026-08-12

The Strait of Hormuz has been closed without ever being formally closed. RUSI analysts describe the situation with a precision that makes the old mental model obsolete. Iran never had to mine the waterway or sustain a blockade. A targeted campaign of missile and drone strikes against commercial vessels, limited in number but devastating in their effect on market psychology, collapsed transit volumes by roughly 90 percent. The traffic that remains sails north around Larak Island, under Iranian supervision, on a route chosen and enforced by Tehran. Around 60 percent of it is Iranian-flagged, Iranian-owned, or tied to Iranian trade. The world’s most important waterway is, in every practical sense, Iranian-controlled.

Access has simply become a political product. That distinction is the story.

The New Model: Access on Political Terms

Under the old rules, a strait used for international navigation is open. Ships go through, whoever they carry and wherever they are from. Iran’s newly created Persian Gulf Strait Authority changes the question. It says permission is required to sail the strait and charges fees of up to two million dollars a passage. Ships seeking permission must file a form stating cargo and destination and, beyond that, the flag, the registered owner and manager, and the nationalities of the crew. Passage is granted selectively: vessels linked to allies like Russia and China, or with ties to Iran like India and Pakistan, move; the rest wait and reroute, or they stop sailing altogether.

As the Brookings analysts Samantha Gross and Ryan Beane argue, this is not a blockade and it is not the old coercion. Iran has introduced a model in which access to a vital shipping route depends on political alignment rather than the established norms of open transit. “States do not need to stop trade to gain influence over it,” they write. Selective access imposes costs on some vessels while conferring advantage on others, and that is the point.

The old picture of maritime conflict assumed two clear outcomes: passage or blockade. The new reality is a third thing, passage as a privilege. It costs less than closure, and it pays for itself while denying the other side a clean casus belli.

The Playbook for Gating a Commons

The Hormuz model did not come from nowhere. RAND’s work on coercive quarantine shows the mechanism in its purest form. A quarantine of Taiwan, RAND’s analysts write, would not need to win a war. It would need only to demonstrate de facto sovereignty by controlling the air and maritime space around the island and the cargo that moves through it. Below the threshold of armed conflict, a power can quietly establish that access to a place runs on its terms. The same playbook has been running in the South China Sea for years, where naval militia and civilian hulls slowly convert contested waters into controlled waters.

The strategic commons, the open sea that maritime powers assume they hold, turns out to be a collection of gates. Most of the time the gates stand open and the assumption holds. A power that learns to work the gates selectively, at the margin, gets leverage without a war. Beijing has absorbed the same lesson, which is why it hedges its dependence on the Strait of Malacca with strategic reserves and overland alternatives even as it retains the ability to threaten chokepoints in a crisis.

Why Hormuz Won’t Stay an Exception

War on the Rocks asked five experts to consider what the Hormuz playbook means for the Strait of Malacca, the Taiwan Strait, the Panama Canal, the Suez Canal, and the Turkish Straits. The exercise itself is the story. Every major chokepoint now carries a price and a list of who may pass.

The Hudson Institute’s Patrick Cronin put the mechanism in a single image. Chokepoints reverse the Venturi effect. A narrow passage that normally accelerates flow becomes, under military threat, a place where traffic slows or stops, magnifying gray-zone coercion and wartime cost. The same geography that makes a strait valuable makes it exploitable.

The deeper point is that the norm was already fraying before this crisis. The United States and China have been at odds over influence in the Panama Canal, and China continues to assert expansive claims in the South China Sea against adverse rulings. Brookings observes that competing interpretations of maritime rights, backed by coercive efforts to enforce them, are eroding freedom of navigation as a public good. Hormuz did not invent the idea that a waterway’s access could be a political lever. It made the idea cheap to exercise.

The Old Frame Still Holds the Field

The classical strategists saw this coming, in outline. Mahan grounded sea power in commerce and the protection of commerce; a navy exists because someone’s trade must pass. Spykman placed the contest on the rimland, the coastal crescent where sea and land powers meet, and every chokepoint on the map sits in that crescent. Sarah Paine’s distinction between sea power and land power is really a distinction about whose geography rewards open access and whose rewards control of a passage. The gated seas are not a new war. They are the oldest war, fought with new instruments at the narrow points where geography concentrates it.

None of this requires abandoning the maritime-strategy frame. It requires updating what command of the sea means. Command, in the old sense, was the ability to sink the other side’s fleet. The Hormuz playbook shows that a state can erode the substance of open transit without ever risking its fleet, by conditioning access one vessel at a time. The global commons, the shared ocean that no state owns but everyone depends on, does not get seized. It gets billed.

The Honest Counterargument

The strongest case against the gated-seas thesis deserves its own hearing, and it is substantial.

First, the tolls are cheap. Brookings estimates that even the two-million-dollar passage fee adds roughly a dollar a barrel to oil, and markets absorb a dollar. Shipping has rerouted around disruptions before through longer routes and bigger inventories; it will again. The world, Brookings concludes, is unlikely to abandon open maritime commerce. Gates add friction. They do not add barriers.

Second, coercion at the gate has been beaten back before. In 1987 Iran was attacking tankers in the Gulf, and Kuwait asked for help. The United States agreed to reflag eleven Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag, and the Navy escorted them through what became, by USNI Proceedings’ account, one of the largest surface warfare operations since World War II. It was costly and improvised. The first convoy took a mine in the Bridgeton, and the Navy had to scramble mine countermeasures into theater. The limits of that success deserve equal weight: under the rules of engagement then in force, protection covered only nationally flagged vessels, leaving most third-world tankers vulnerable. USNI’s own 1988 assessment called it an expensive stalemate. The United States kept the reflagged tankers moving. It did not restore open transit for everyone.

Third, and this is the point the hawks usually miss, reopening a gated strait by force is not a solved problem. RUSI’s analysis of the current campaign is blunt. No military operation can guarantee that Iranian strikes stop; one or two successful strikes a day may be the irreducible cost of sustained naval operations in those waters; only a diplomatic settlement holds out the prospect of halting attacks entirely. The strait is roughly thirty kilometers wide, about the diameter of the drone kill-zones that have defined attrition in Ukraine. The escort that reassures the insurance market is also the target-rich environment that makes the mission most dangerous.

Which is why the real gate is not naval. RUSI puts the metric plainly: the strait will be functionally open again when war-risk underwriters are willing to insure vessels transiting it at rates that make passage economically viable. Command of the sea, in this telling, has to be re-earned continuously, in the insurance market as much as on the water. That is the mechanism by which the gated seas get un-gated, and it is economic, not martial.

The Real Stake: Normalization

The danger, both Brookings and RUSI warn, is not that Hormuz stays closed. It is that politically conditioned access becomes normal, and normal becomes precedent. Once access is tied to alignment, every state that borders a chokepoint has a reason to try it, and every great power has a reason to build its own competing system of privileged access, its own allied corridors and favored-flag lanes. The United States is already at risk of playing that game, coordinating the transit of favored vessels, under pressure to tie protection to alliances and, in RUSI’s telling, incentivized to restrict traffic while Iran is incentivized to encourage it on its own terms. That inversion is the new equilibrium the great powers are sliding toward. It is a world where openness is not the default but a thing to be bought and bargained, or extorted.

The response that preserves open transit is the response that keeps contingent access unattractive. That means contesting the practice, not merely the effect. It means keeping the routes open at the margins where they are being conditioned. And it means bearing the cost of security jointly enough that no single power is tempted to sell its protection for loyalty. Brookings’ prescription is short and unfashionable: do no harm. Avoid the deal that legitimizes politically differentiated access. Resist the competing systems of privileged access. Keep credible signals that excessive claims will be contested.

Conclusion

The great-power argument has always been about access to the global commons, the shared ocean lanes that let a coalition trade and feed itself long after a land empire has spent its own coerced surplus. That was the bet in the last post, and it holds. But the commons are not a standing asset. They are a recurring cost, and the account is now being billed at the gates.

Sea power was never the ability to win one battle. It was the ability to keep the water open. The gated seas test that ability in a new form, where the enemy does not need to sink the fleet to close the route. Economic elasticity, the capacity to keep paying for the war after the initial push stalls, still decides who outlasts whom. What changed is that the price of open water is now set by whoever controls the gate. The question for the maritime coalition is whether it is willing to pay that price in the form that keeps the seas open rather than the form that keeps them gated. Command of the sea doesn’t conquer. It endures, but only if the water stays open.
